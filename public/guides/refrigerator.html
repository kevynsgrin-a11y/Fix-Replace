Refrigerator guide

Refrigerator: repair or replace?

The fridge is the one appliance that never gets to rest, and the one where a single expensive fault — a sealed-system leak — can rival the price of a new unit. Here’s how to tell a cheap fix from a write-off.

Data reviewed July 19, 2026 · BLS OEWS 49-9031 · EIA residential rates

Refrigerator repairs split cleanly into two worlds. On one side are inexpensive, often DIY-friendly parts — a fan motor, a door gasket, a water inlet valve — that are almost always worth fixing. On the other side is the sealed refrigeration system: the compressor and refrigerant circuit, whose repair can run $500–$1,250 and legally requires an EPA-certified technician. The whole decision usually turns on which world your fault lives in, and how old the unit is.

Refrigerant work is a safety hard-stop

Handling refrigerant legally requires EPA Section 608 certification. We never show DIY parts or tutorials for compressor, refrigerant, or sealed-system repairs — those go to a certified pro. See the safety hard-stops.

When to repair vs when to replace

Forget the crude “repair if it’s under half the price of new.” A better rule folds in the unit’s age and the odds of a second failure:

SituationLean
Cheap fault (gasket, fan, valve, board) on a freestanding fridge under ~8 yearsRepair
Sealed-system / compressor fault on a freestanding fridge over ~10 yearsUsually replace
Any repair under ~$500 on a unit within its lifespan bandRepair
Built-in unit — almost any single major repairRepair

Why does a built-in flip the answer? Because replacing one is a $6,500–$11,000 project plus roughly $800 to install, so even a top-of-band sealed-system repair is easily the cheaper path. A freestanding fridge with a $1,250 sealed-system quote is the opposite story — that’s near the value of a new $1,400 mid-range unit, and once you add repeat-failure risk and about $440 in delivery, install, and haul-away for the new one, replacing often wins.

Don’t forget the energy gap

An aging refrigerator uses roughly 800 kWh/year versus about 400 kWh/year for a modern ENERGY STAR model. That ~400 kWh saving is about $71/year at the national average rate and around $106/year in a high-rate state like New York (26.49 ¢/kWh) — which shortens the payback on replacing.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth repairing a refrigerator?

It depends on the fault and the age. A cheap, DIY-friendly fault like an evaporator fan motor ($180–$280) or door gasket ($100–$250) on a unit under about 8 years old is almost always worth repairing. A sealed-system leak or compressor failure ($500–$1,250) on a freestanding fridge past 10 years usually favors replacement once repeat-failure risk, disposal, and energy are counted.

How long does a refrigerator last?

A freestanding refrigerator lasts about 8–11 years at the budget tier, 11–15 years mid-range, and 15–20 years premium. Built-in units run longer: roughly 12–15 years mid-range and 18–25 years premium.

How much does it cost to fix a refrigerator that won’t cool?

It depends on the cause. An evaporator fan motor runs $180–$280 installed; a refrigerant leak or recharge $250–$900; a compressor $300–$650; and a full sealed-system repair $500–$1,250. Sealed-system and refrigerant work legally requires an EPA-certified technician.

Why do built-in refrigerators justify more expensive repairs?

Because replacement is far more expensive. A new built-in refrigerator starts around $4,200 and runs to $11,000, plus roughly $800 in installation, so even a $1,250 sealed-system repair is usually the rational choice on a built-in that a freestanding fridge would not justify.
