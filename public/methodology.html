Methodology

Every number has a source.

A financial tool is only as trustworthy as its inputs. This page documents where each input comes from, how we turn public data into localized estimates, and the principle we hold above all others: we never invent precision the data can’t support.

From public data to a local estimate

Labor: a wage becomes a multiplier

BLS publishes an area mean hourly wage for appliance repairers. We divide the local mean by the national mean baseline ($24.10/hr) to get a multiplier, clamped to 0.85–1.50 so no single metro can distort an estimate. Because a repair is part parts and part labor, we only scale the labor share:

costadj = cost · ( 1 − laborShare ) + cost · laborShare · multiplier

A few published anchors ground the scale: the national median wage is $22.68/hr and the 90th percentile $34.93/hr; Minneapolis–St. Paul averages $28.51/hr and Miami $22.87/hr — which is why the identical repair is genuinely cheaper in Miami than in Minneapolis, and why a “fair” price in one metro can look like gouging in another.

Energy: state rates, real efficiency deltas

Operating cost is annual consumption times the local rate. State electricity rates range widely — from around 11 ¢/kWh in Washington to 26.49 ¢/kWh in New York and higher still in Hawaii — so the same worn refrigerator costs meaningfully more to keep running in a high-rate state, shortening the payback on an efficient replacement.

Lifespans: bands, not point estimates

We never publish “your appliance will last 12.4 years.” Lifespans are bands (e.g. a mid-range dishwasher at 9–12 years, a premium one at 15–20), and the Weibull fit turns those bands into survival probabilities. The math is set out in the model walkthrough.

The no-fake-precision principle

The most common failure mode of a decision tool is confident nonsense. We guard against it on purpose:

1

Suppress when the data is sparse

Missing the failed part, the age, or the location lowers the confidence score and widens — not narrows — the reported range. A low-confidence result says so plainly.

2

Suppress when a quote is implausible

A quote more than double the regional band withholds the verdict entirely and flags a likely predatory price, instead of laundering a bad number into a clean recommendation.

3

Ranges, not false decimals

We present cost bands and probability ranges. A single dollar figure to the cent would imply a certainty the underlying public data does not have.

4

Safety overrides economics

Hazardous repairs are routed to a professional no matter how favorable the dollar math looks. See the safety hard-stops.

A note on estimates

These figures are decision-grade estimates from public, national datasets — enough to make a confident repair-or-replace call, not a substitute for a technician’s on-site diagnosis. Actual quotes vary with brand, model, part availability, and the specific fault. Read the terms for the full disclaimer.
