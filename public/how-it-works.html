Why the “50% rule” fails

The popular rule says: if the repair costs more than 50% of a new unit, replace it. It is easy to remember and almost always wrong at the margin, because it collapses a multi-variable decision into one ratio and quietly assumes several things that are rarely true:

  • It ignores age. A $300 repair on a 3-year-old washer and a 13-year-old washer are not the same decision, yet the 50% rule scores them identically.
  • It ignores the risk of a second failure. An old machine that fails once is far more likely to fail again soon — the rule never prices that in.
  • It ignores energy. Running an aging unit for five more years in a high-rate state can quietly cost more than the repair itself.
  • It ignores the true cost of buying new. Delivery, installation, and hauling the old unit away are real dollars the sticker price hides.
  • It ignores where you live. The same repair is priced very differently in Miami and Boston because field labor rates differ.
  • It ignores whether the quote is even fair. Feed it a gouging quote and it will happily tell you to replace a perfectly repairable appliance.

We replace that single ratio with five components that each answer one honest question, then combine them on an equal footing.

1 · Remaining useful life — a Weibull survival model

Instead of announcing that “your dishwasher dies in two years,” we treat lifespan as a probability distribution — standard practice in reliability engineering — and report the odds of surviving the next one, two, and three years. The engine uses a two-parameter Weibull distribution, whose reliability function is:

R(t) = exp( −( t / η )k )

We fit the two parameters from each appliance tier’s published lifespan band by treating the band’s low and high years as the 25th and 75th percentiles of the failure distribution — one defensible assumption that pins both parameters:

shape k = ln( ln4 / ln(4/3) )ln( high / low )   ·   scale η = low(−ln 0.75)1/k

A tighter band implies a larger shape (more predictable wear-out); a wider band implies more variance. The shape is clamped to a physically sensible wear-out range of 1.4–8. What we actually need is conditional survival — the chance the unit lasts another d years given it has already reached age a:

R(a+d | a) = exp( (a/η)k − ((a+d)/η)k )

And the median remaining life m — the point at which the unit has a 50/50 chance of still running — is found by solving R(a+m | a) = 0.5:

m = η · ( (a/η)k + ln 2 )1/k − a

Because the Weibull hazard rate rises after the median lifespan, this gives exactly the age penalty a good decision needs — and, importantly, it flows from one actuarial model rather than a pile of separate guesses.

Where the lifespan bands come from

The tier bands are refined from the NAHB Study of Life Expectancy of Home Components and the InterNACHI Estimated Life Expectancy Chart. A mid-range freestanding refrigerator, for example, carries an 11–15 year band; a premium one, 15–20 years. See the methodology for the full provenance table.

2 · Expected repair cost, not just the quote

Paying a repair bill on an aging machine buys you an uncertain amount of service, because the other components are just as old. We therefore compare against a risk-adjusted repair cost:

E[Crepair] = Cquote + Prepeat · Csubsequent
  • CquoteThe repair price you were quoted (your number, entered as-is).
  • PrepeatProbability of another significant failure within two years — read straight off the same Weibull curve as 1 − R(a+2 | a), capped at 0.85 so a very old unit never implies a certain second failure.
  • CsubsequentThe cost of a typical next major repair for that category, regionally adjusted.

Tying Prepeat to the survival model means the penalty for keeping an old machine emerges from the physics of wear-out, not a second opinion bolted on afterward. A young appliance barely moves the number; a machine well past its median lifespan sees its effective repair cost climb sharply.

3 · Net Present Cost over a common horizon

Repairing and replacing buy different amounts of future service, so summing each over its own lifespan is apples-to-oranges. We fix a common horizon H — the expected lifespan of the new unit — and model equivalent service on both paths, discounting every future dollar monthly at a macro opportunity-cost-of-capital rate (default 5%):

Replace now

Buy new today. It reaches end-of-life exactly at horizon H (zero residual). You pay its sticker price plus delivery, installation, and haul-away — then run it on efficient energy for the whole horizon.

Repair & run

Pay E[Crepair] and keep the old unit for its remaining life Rold. If that is shorter than H, buy a replacement at year Rold and run it to H — crediting the still-young unit its terminal residual value.

Each future cash flow is discounted with a monthly factor derived from the annual rate r:

discount(month) = 1( 1 + rm )month   where  rm = ( 1 + r )1/12 − 1

The two paths’ present values are compared directly, and the break-even month is the first month at which replacing-now’s cumulative cost falls at or below the repair path’s. This is how a high electricity rate — New York residential power runs 26.49 ¢/kWh, roughly 49% above the national average — can flip a decision: the operating savings of an efficient replacement compound month after month until they overtake the repair.

4 · Confidence & the predatory-quote guard

A precise-looking verdict built on thin inputs is worse than an honest “we’re not sure.” Every result carries a confidence score that starts at 100 and is docked for missing information — no identified failed part (−25), no appliance age (−20), no location (−10) — and mapped to a level of high (≥80), moderate (≥60), or low.

The most important job of this layer is to catch a gouging quote. We compare your quote to the regional cost band for that specific repair. If it lands above twice the top of the band, we suppress the verdict entirely rather than let a bad number produce a confident “replace it”:

if  Cquote > 2.0 × bandhigh  →  verdict suppressed — “get a second opinion”

A worked example

A dishwasher drain-pump replacement runs about $150–$320 installed nationally. If a shop quotes $900 for that job, the number is more than double the top of the band. Rather than tell you to replace a repairable dishwasher, the model withholds a verdict and flags the quote as likely predatory — the honest answer is “that price is the problem, get another quote.”

Quotes that are merely high (above 1.4× the band) dent confidence and are noted; quotes that are suspiciously low (below 35% of the band’s floor) trigger a “confirm this covers parts and labor” warning. The goal throughout is no fake precision.

5 · Safety hard-stops

Economics never override safety. Certain repairs carry real physical risk, and for those the model refuses to show DIY parts links or tutorials and routes you to a licensed professional — regardless of what the dollar math says:

High voltageCapacitors (e.g. a microwave’s) hold a lethal charge even unplugged.Pro only
RefrigerantHandling refrigerant legally requires EPA Section 608 certification.Pro only

Any internal microwave repair is treated as high-voltage by category, no matter which part failed. And a component that simply isn’t homeowner-serviceable (a washer transmission, say) is routed to a pro even when it carries no safety hazard — there is no safe DIY path, so we don’t pretend there is one.

Putting it together: the verdict

With those five components computed independently, the verdict is the sign and size of the Net Present Cost gap — repair minus replace. A gap near zero is reported honestly as “it’s close.” A live safety recall or an active warranty pulls the recommendation firmly toward repair, because in those cases the manufacturer typically owes you a free remedy. Paid links, if any, are appended after the verdict is fixed and are never allowed to influence it.

The one-line summary

We estimate how much longer your appliance will realistically last, what a repair truly costs once repeat-failure risk is priced in, and compare that head-to-head with buying new over the same time horizon — in today’s dollars, at your local rates — and we tell you when the data isn’t good enough to be sure.
